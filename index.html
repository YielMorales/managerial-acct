Cost accumulation systems

A cost accumulation (product costing) system collects DM, DL and factory overhead and assigns them to cost objects. It is needed for inventory valuation and income determination (external) and for setting prices (internal). Two steps: measure the costs incurred, then assign them to products.

  • Job-order costing: costs traced to a specific job, order or contract (this session).
  • Process costing: costs accumulated by department and averaged over homogeneous output (Session 6).
  • Activity-based costing: overhead assigned through many activity pools and drivers (Session 7).

All three assign the same three cost elements (DM, DL, OH); they differ only in how the costs are traced and assigned.

Job-order costing also works for services: the “job” is a client engagement, case file or repair order; professional hours are DL, filing fees/parts are DM, and office rent/admin/IT are overhead applied with a predetermined rate.

The job cost sheet

Records the DM, DL and applied OH for one job. The file of job cost sheets for unfinished jobs is the subsidiary ledger for Work-in-Process; completed sheets move to a completed-jobs file supporting Finished Goods.

  • Materials requisition form → direct materials issued to the job.
  • Time ticket → hours a worker spent on the job and the wage rate.
  • Predetermined overhead rate → overhead applied using the job's actual activity.

Chiphard Works, Job 123: DM 1,200 + 650 + 350 = ₱2,200; DL (180 × 6.50) + (140 × 7.25) = ₱2,185; OH 320 hrs × ₱4.50 = ₱1,440; total ₱5,825. Price at 40% markup on cost = 5,825 × 1.40 = ₱8,155.

Predetermined overhead rate (POHR)

POHR = Budgeted annual overhead ÷ Budgeted annual activity Applied OH = POHR × ACTUAL activity used by the job

Overhead can't be traced like DM/DL: much of it is incurred for the whole factory and period, and actual totals aren't known until period-end. A budgeted rate lets you cost and price a job during the period: Session 1's timeliness-over-precision trade-off.

Activity base options: direct labor hours (labor-intensive), machine hours (automated), direct labor cost (when wage rates vary). Pick the base most correlated with what drives overhead. For a highly automated line, use machine hours.

Quezon Print Solutions: 2,700,000 ÷ 45,000 DLH = ₱60/DLH. Job #302 = 62,000 + (180 × 90 = 16,200) + (180 × 60 = 10,800) = ₱89,000.

Normal costing (standard practice): DM and DL at actual, OH applied with the predetermined rate. Actual costing uses actual OH, so it's only known at period-end and too slow for pricing.

Under- and overapplied overhead

Underapplied: Applied OH < Actual OH (jobs were undercharged) Overapplied : Applied OH > Actual OH (jobs were overcharged)
  • Immaterial → close entirely to COGS. Underapplied: Dr COGS / Cr Factory OH (COGS goes up). Overapplied: Dr Factory OH / Cr COGS (COGS goes down).
  • Material → prorate across WIP, FG and COGS in proportion to the applied overhead in each account's ending balance.

Company X: ₱2/MH × 96,000 MH = 192,000 applied vs 198,000 actual → ₱6,000 underapplied. Company Y: 150% × 176,000 = 264,000 applied vs 256,000 actual → ₱8,000 overapplied.

Cavite Precision Parts proration (₱60,000 overapplied): WIP 20% → ↓12,000; FG 30% → ↓18,000; COGS 50% → ↓30,000. If it were underapplied, all three would be increases.

Pampanga Metal Works: POHR 4,500,000 ÷ 30,000 = ₱150. Applied = 29,500 × 150 = 4,425,000 vs actual 4,650,000 → ₱225,000 underapplied, COGS increased.

Departmental vs plantwide rates

A single plantwide rate blends labor-driven and machine-driven departments into one average. Departmental rates use each department's own base. Example: Dept A 72,000 ÷ 64,000 DL cost = 112.5% of DL cost; Dept B 75,000 ÷ 10,000 MH = ₱7.50/MH. Job 105 (30 units) = ₱289.00 under departmental rates (₱9.63/unit) vs ₱245.80 under a 180%-of-DL-cost plantwide rate. The plantwide rate undercosts this machine-heavy job by ₱43.20, previewing ABC.

EVPI (side topic in the deck)

EVPI = EPPI − EMV EPPI = expected payoff if you always knew the true state in advance EMV = expected value of your best choice under current uncertainty

Information only has value if it could change your decision. EVPI is the ceiling on what perfect information is worth; real (imperfect) research is worth less, so paying more than EVPI is irrational.

The big picture

Process costing accumulates costs by department and divides by output, so every unit gets an average cost. It fits continuous, homogeneous, high-volume output: refining, chemicals, textiles, food and beverage, cement. Gallon #4,001 is indistinguishable from #4,002, so there is nothing unique to trace.

Only two cost categories: direct materials and conversion costs (DL + OH combined, since they're incurred together and evenly). Job-order costing keeps all three separate.

Hybrid / operation costing: a shoe maker can use process costing for standard cutting/stitching and job-order costing for a custom limited batch. The choice depends on whether the output is unique or homogeneous, department by department.

The five-step framework

  1. Physical units: Beginning WIP + Units started = Units completed + Ending WIP.
  2. Equivalent units (separately for materials and conversion).
  3. Total costs to account for, by category.
  4. Unit cost = total cost ÷ equivalent units, per category, then summed.
  5. Assign costs to units transferred out and to ending WIP, and reconcile to Step 3.

All five steps appear on the cost-of-production report: one report per department, per period (vs one job cost sheet per job). The physical flow equation also helps detect lost units (spoilage, shrinkage).

Equivalent units (EU)

EU = how many whole units the completed plus partially completed units represent. 100 units 60% complete = 60 EU.

  • Materials at the start: any unit in process is 100% complete for materials.
  • Evenly throughout: same % as conversion.
  • At the end: ending WIP is 0% complete for materials (so materials EU can be lower than conversion EU).
  • Units completed and transferred out are always 100% complete for every category.

Common trap: applying the same % to materials and conversion. Always ask, separately, “when is this cost added?”

Weighted-average method

WA EU = Units completed + (Ending WIP × % complete) Unit cost = (Beginning WIP cost + Current cost) ÷ WA EU

Blends last period's cost in beginning WIP with this period's cost into one average. Beginning WIP's % never enters the formula. Simpler and most common in practice; best when costs are stable. Weakness: an efficiency gain or loss this period gets diluted by last period's costs.

Nueva Ecija Rice Mill: EU materials 16,000 + 4,000 = 20,000; conversion 16,000 + 1,000 = 17,000. Materials (18,000 + 162,000) ÷ 20,000 = ₱9; conversion (9,600 + 143,400) ÷ 17,000 = ₱9. Transferred out 16,000 × 18 = ₱288,000; ending WIP ₱45,000; total ₱333,000 ✓.

FIFO method

FIFO EU = WA EU − (Beginning WIP × % already done last period) = Units completed + (Ending WIP × %) − (Beginning WIP × % done) Unit cost = CURRENT-period cost only ÷ FIFO EU

Keeps beginning WIP cost separate, assuming it is finished and transferred out first. FIFO EU are never larger than WA EU. Better for cost control because it isolates this period's performance. Use when input prices are volatile (Iloilo Sugar Refinery's price spike → FIFO).

Department A: WA EU 6,500 / 6,100 → FIFO 6,500 − 1,500 = 5,000 materials; 6,100 − 1,000 = 5,100 conversion. Aklan Fish Cannery (materials at end, beg WIP 0% materials): FIFO materials EU = WA = 9,000; conversion 9,800 − 700 = 9,100.

FIFO cost transferred out = Beginning WIP cost + Cost to complete beginning WIP (its remaining %) + Units started and completed × full current unit cost.

Multiple departments and transferred-in cost

Each department prepares its own report. Cost completed in an earlier department becomes transferred-in cost in the next, treated like material added at the start of the receiving department, so it is always 100% complete. A department can have three EU columns: transferred-in, materials, conversion.

When the report doesn't reconcile

Work backward in this order: re-check Step 1's physical balance (cheapest), then Step 2's equivalent units (wrong % is the most likely cause), then Step 4's division.

Why traditional costing breaks down

Traditional systems apply overhead with one volume-based rate (DLH or MH), assuming every product consumes overhead in proportion to that measure. Automation shrank direct labor and grew overhead, so that assumption no longer reflects cause and effect.

Cost distortion: systematic overcosting of some products and undercosting of others.

  • High-volume, simple products → typically overcosted.
  • Low-volume, complex products (many setups, inspections, special handling) → typically undercosted.
  • This is cross-subsidization: the simple product subsidizes the complex one.

Warning signs: competitors keep underpricing your simplest high-volume product, while your premium custom product barely breaks even despite a big markup.

What ABC is

ABC assigns overhead based on a product's use of activities, not volume alone. It is not an alternative to job-order or process costing; it is a more refined way to assign overhead within either. The key difference is the number of cost drivers. Stage 1 (traditional): costs to departments; Stage 1 (ABC): costs to activities; Stage 2 (both): to products.

  1. Identify activities and their cost drivers.
  2. Compute an activity rate for each pool.
  3. Sum the activity costs each product consumes.
Activity rate = Budgeted activity-pool cost ÷ Predicted driver level Overhead assigned = Σ (Activity rate × Driver units the product used)

Building pools (regrouping overhead accounts by activity via interviews, time studies, logs) is the hard part; the arithmetic is easy. A driver must be chosen because it causes the cost, not because its data is convenient.

The cost hierarchy

  • Unit-level: every unit (machining one part). Volume drivers work fine here.
  • Batch-level: once per batch, any size (setup).
  • Product-level: supports a product line (engineering changes, formulas, regulatory filings).
  • Facility-level: keeps the plant running (factory rent, top management). Often left unallocated.

Match each pool to a driver from the same level. Forcing batch/product costs onto a volume driver is the traditional-costing mistake.

Services and customers

Same three steps. Cavite Medical Diagnostic Center: rates scheduling ₱20/visit, lab ₱30/procedure, consultation ₱60/hour → Basic Panel ₱56, Imaging ₱80, Executive Package ₱110 per visit. Drivers elsewhere: bed-days (hospitals), takeoffs/landings (airlines), rooms occupied (hotels), teller transactions and loan applications (banks).

Rizal Wholesale customers: ₱100/order, ₱200/delivery → A ₱80,000 profit, B (₱140,000), C ₱20,000. B's many small orders and deliveries eat more than its gross margin.

Benefits, limits and the adoption decision

  • Benefits: more accurate costs; reveals the cost of complexity and small batches; better pricing, mix and bidding; highlights non-value-added activity (bridge to ABM).
  • Limits: costly to design and maintain; heavy data collection; activity rates are still estimates; may not pay off in simple, labor-intensive, low-overhead firms.
  • Adopt when: overhead is large relative to direct cost, products are diverse in volume/complexity/batch size, activities beyond volume are significant, and decisions have real stakes.

Single-product bottled-water plant → stay traditional. Custom electronics with 40+ variants → adopt ABC. One product with constant batch sizes (Iloilo Precision Tools) → ABC ≈ plantwide rate. Many firms pilot ABC on one line, or use 4–6 well-chosen pools.

From ABC to ABM

ABM uses activity-based cost information to improve operations: eliminate non-value-added activities, redesign processes, and understand true product, customer and supplier profitability.

Accurate costs alone change nothing until management acts. ABM is cyclical, like Kaizen (continuous improvement): measure → identify waste → diagnose cause → act → re-measure. It's cross-functional: operations, sales, procurement.

Process-value analysis (PVA)

Classify every activity as value-added or non-value-added from the customer's view. Test: would the customer pay for it if they could see it?

  • Value-added: transforms the product (cutting, machining, assembly, sanding/finishing, painting).
  • Non-value-added: scheduling, moving, waiting, inspecting, storing.
  • Necessary ≠ value-added. Inspection is often necessary but still non-value-added; a perfect product needs none. Prevention reduces the need for appraisal and failure.
  • Quality costs: prevention mostly value-added; internal/external failure entirely non-value-added; appraisal sits in between.

Responses to non-value-added activities: Eliminate, Reduce, Select (cheapest way to do a necessary one), Share (spread over more output). Non-value-added time often eats 60–90% of cycle time in traditional plants.

JIT: demand-pull production; inventory is a symptom of hidden problems. Manufacturing cells group machines by product family, cutting moving and waiting; workers become multifunction labor.

Driver analysis and root cause

Asks why an activity happens and this often, not just how much it costs. Custom Bracket's 800 setups/year are a symptom; root causes could be small batches (set minimum order quantities), slow changeovers (SMED setup reduction) or poor scheduling. Each cause implies a different fix.

  • 5 Whys (Toyota): ask “why?” repeatedly. The setup-cost chain ends at a sales policy (no minimum order), far from the factory floor.
  • Fishbone diagram (Kaoru Ishikawa, 1960s): six cause categories — processes, equipment, materials, people, environment, management.
  • Kepner-Tregoe: situation appraisal (what's going on?), problem analysis (why?), decision analysis (what should we do?), potential problem/opportunity analysis (what lies ahead?).
  • Data analytics: regress cost on candidate drivers, but watch for multicollinearity (Session 3) when candidate causes are correlated with each other.

Customer profitability

Gross margin ignores selling and service cost. Assign order-processing and delivery costs with ABC. Rizal Wholesale: ranked by GM A > B > C; by operating profit A > C > B (B loses ₱140,000).

Responses: renegotiate terms (minimum orders, consolidated deliveries), activity-based pricing, work with the customer to change behavior, and only as a last resort drop them (strategic value matters). If B consolidates to 250 orders and 300 deliveries: cost 25,000 + 60,000 = ₱85,000 → profit ₱55,000.

Whale curve: a few customers generate more than 100% of profit while a long tail loses money. Use it to tier customers (protect, monitor, renegotiate).

Supplier costing: total cost of ownership (TCO)

TCO = Purchase price + hidden activity costs (receiving, inspection, rework, expediting, administrative)

Supplier A at ₱95 vs B at ₱100: A's hidden costs of ₱31,000 per 1,000 units vs B's ₱5,000 make A's TCO ₱126/unit vs B's ₱105. Choose on TCO, not price. Zamboanga Seafood: defects up from 1% to 4% raised TCO from ₱3.9M to ₱4.88M (+₱980,000) with no change in price. JIT purchasing makes supplier quality critical because there's no buffer stock.

Activity performance measures

Cycle time = Processing + Inspection + Moving + Waiting MCE = Value-added (processing) time ÷ Cycle time Velocity = Units produced ÷ Cycle time

Marikina Shoe: 3.5 + 1.0 + 4.5 + 1.0 = 10 hrs → MCE 35%. Cells cut waiting to 1.5 hrs → cycle 7 hrs → MCE 50%. 100-unit order: 10 days → 4 days: MCE 20% → 50%, velocity 10 → 25 units/day. Cycle time is the denominator of both, so removing waste raises both. Traditional plants often run MCE below 50%; world-class JIT reaches 80–90%. MCE is a Balanced Scorecard internal-process measure.
